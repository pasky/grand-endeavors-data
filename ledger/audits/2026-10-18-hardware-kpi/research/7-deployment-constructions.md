# Robots deployment KPI: constructions and practical estimators

**Research cut-off: 17 October 2026.** Scope is general-purpose autonomous physical automation (e.g. humanoids/mobile manipulators that could be redeployed), not robot software, robotaxis, or purpose-built cells. “Deployment” should mean productive work actually performed, not capability, shipment, purchase, or a supervised demo. The record is poor: no public, recurring series reports robot-hours × autonomy × task output for this scope. All values below distinguish published evidence from illustrative assumptions.

## What existing measures do—and do not—measure

| Approach | Measure and useful lesson | Limit for our KPI |
|---|---|---|
| **Acemoglu–Restrepo robot exposure** | In *Robots and Jobs: Evidence from US Labor Markets* (2017 WP; 2020 JPE), combine IFR industry robot stocks/installations with industry employment to construct local exposure: industry robot penetration (robots per worker) times local industry employment shares. Their US design uses IFR robot data across industries/countries and commuting-zone employment shares; the instrument uses other countries’ industry robot adoption. It is a realized stock/diffusion proxy, unlike a capability score. They estimate exposure’s labor-market effects, not the share of work autonomously done. [NBER paper](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w23285/w23285.pdf); [published article record](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/jep.34.1.3) (the NBER WP is the source for construction). | IFR mostly covers multipurpose industrial robots under a formal definition, not GP robots; a robot count is not autonomous hours or displaced tasks. Worker reallocation, output growth and task redesign confound employment outcomes. Industry/occupation crosswalk is coarse; annual/periodic data lag. **Autonomy not verified.** |
| **Task displacement / task approach** | Acemoglu & Restrepo’s task framework separates displacement (capital takes tasks) from reinstatement (new labor tasks); employment effects are not a direct measure of robot work. Robot exposure studies use tasks or industry adoption to estimate displacement. [“Tasks, Automation, and the Rise in US Wage Inequality”](https://www.nber.org/papers/w28920) (2021) develops the task framework. This cautions against inferring deployment from job losses: robots can perform work without reducing headcount, or employment can fall for other reasons. | Requires observed task reallocation or labor outcomes plus counterfactual; indirect and lagged. No robot-hours/autonomy numerator. |
| **Comin–Hobijn CHAT / diffusion** | CHAT (Cross-country Historical Adoption of Technology) assembles long time series of adoption for many technologies/countries. Comin & Hobijn distinguish **extensive margin** (when adoption starts / share adopting) from **intensive margin** (penetration or quantity in use conditional on adoption). For robots, the lesson is to track both firm/site adoption and intensity per adopter (working units, shifts, throughput), not merely the share with any robot. [CHAT data](https://datahub.io/technology/historical-adoption-of-technology); [“An Exploration of Technology Diffusion”](https://dcomin.host.dartmouth.edu/files/exploration_technology.pdf) (2010). | Historical technology series have varying definitions and often measure installed stock, not service-hours. No standardized GP robot definition or autonomy audit. |
| **Anthropic Economic Index (AEI)** | Actual Claude usage is a usage-based deployment proxy: it samples/classifies real conversations/tasks and maps observed work tasks to O*NET; coverage is task-weighted observed usage, rather than theoretical model capability alone. In the March 2026 labor-market paper Anthropic explicitly combines theoretical exposure with observed Claude work-related usage; it uses August/November 2025 conversation datasets and notes sampling thresholds and task-mapping judgments. [AEI](https://www.anthropic.com/economic-index); [labor-market methodology/report, 5 Mar 2026](https://www.anthropic.com/research/labor-market-impacts); [task-use study](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.04761). | Conversations ≠ completed productive work, represent Claude rather than all AI, and usage can augment rather than substitute. A robot analogue would log verified autonomous task cycles/hours and task types, normalized by work-task hours—not sales/downloads. Privacy-preserving aggregation plus independent validation would be needed. |
| **Anthropic robot “cost-competitive share”** | Anthropic’s *Can we predict the jobs robots will do?* (published 2026; retrieved 17 Oct 2026) maps O*NET task descriptions to physical-task exposure, time weights and employment. It asks Claude to rate current robot capability and estimates annualized robot cost versus occupation labor compensation (including purchase/capital, integration, maintenance, software, energy, oversight, insurance and end-of-life). Reported headline: robots can perform 74% of physical tasks, covering 34% of US working time, but are cost-competitive for **0.3% of job tasks/work** (reported as about 300,000 jobs in press accounts). The 0.3% is economically feasible potential, not actual usage. [Study](https://www.anthropic.com/research/what-work-can-robots-do). | Cost comparison is modeled and task-level; capability ratings are judgments, not observed work. Cost-competitive task exposure does not show buyers deployed robots or verified autonomy. A 0.3% share should not be labeled deployment. |
| **Webb (2020) patents** | Webb measures occupational AI exposure by semantic overlap between AI patent text and O*NET task descriptions, weighted by occupation task importance. Adapted to robot patents, this can capture the direction/availability of inventions. [Paper](https://www.michaelwebb.co/webb_ai.pdf); [data/site](https://www.michaelwebb.co/). | **Exposure, not adoption**: patents may never ship, may be narrow/non-GP, and omit implementation/use. Useful only as capability/supply-side corroboration. |
| **Epoch AI robot production** | Epoch estimates 2025 production around **16,000 humanoids**, doubling every 6 months (2022–25 trend), plus 81,000 quadrupeds, 570,000 arms, 33m wheeled robots and 16m drones. It stresses tiny/anomalous humanoid base and says most current humanoid purchases are research/marketing rather than productive use. Production constraints and scale-up are analyzed explicitly. [22 Apr 2026](https://epoch.ai/publications/how-fast-could-robot-production-scale-up). | Production is a supply flow, not installed stock, productive use or autonomous work. Form factors include robots outside our GP scope; no US split or autonomy-hours. Excellent leading indicator, weak deployment KPI. |
| **Robotaxi analogy** | A strong template: report paid fully driverless trips/miles (and cities/ODD) divided by all ride-hail/taxi trips or miles in the same geography/time. It combines capability and adoption into an observed service share. Waymo said it passed **500,000 paid rides/week across 10 US cities** in March 2026; the figure is company-reported. [TechCrunch, 27 Mar 2026](https://techcrunch.com/2026/03/27/waymo-skyrocketing-ridership-in-one-chart/). This is analogous to “share of work actually done autonomously.” | Raw rides need matched market denominator, geography, distance and empty/repositioning miles; driverless should exclude safety-driver/remote intervention modes. For robots, count task-hours or human-equivalent output with autonomy verified, and report geography/task denominators. Company figures need audit. |
| **Amazon scale / productivity** | Amazon announced its millionth robot in June 2025 and said robots supported operations at 75% of global deliveries/operations (company phrasing); count demonstrates large-scale purpose-built automation, not GP autonomy. [Amazon announcement](https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/operations/amazon-robotics-robots-fulfillment-center); [GeekWire, 1 Jul 2025](https://www.geekwire.com/2025/amazons-robot-workforce-hits-1-million-heres-what-they-all-do/). LEAP expert panel projects Amazon real net sales per distribution employee rising from **$464,771 (2024)** to $480,000 (2026) and $549,842 (2030). [LEAP Wave 7, 19 Apr 2026](https://leap.forecastingresearch.org/reports/wave7). | Robot fleet counts mix autonomous mobile robots and highly specialized machines; not comparable to GP robots. Sales per employee is affected by mix, prices, outsourcing and throughput, not causal robot productivity. LEAP is a forecast panel, not observed deployment. |
| **IFR robot density** | IFR’s operating industrial-robot stock per 10,000 manufacturing employees: 2023 global average **162**, with 2024 Western Europe 267 and North America 204. [IFR, 20 Nov 2024](https://ifr.org/ifr-press-releases/news/global-robot-density-in-factories-doubled-in-seven-years); [2024 regional release, 8 Apr 2026](https://ifr.org/ifr-press-releases/news/robot-density-surges-in-europe-asia-and-americas); [OWID chart/data](https://ourworldindata.org/grapher/industrial-robots-in-operation-per-1000-employees). | Reliable recurring adoption/density series for traditional industrial automation, but not task-hours, GP units, autonomy or productivity. Can be a benchmark, not the KPI. |

## Candidate “poor man’s” estimators

### A. Capability × observed adoption

For each task *j*, estimate capability share `C_j` (tiered demo / pilot / productive deployed, with GP and autonomy gates), multiply by observed adoption/intensity `A_j`, and weight by task hours `H_j`:

`Deployment share = Σ_j H_j × C_j × A_j / Σ_j H_j`.

Here `A_j` must be the share of task opportunities actually served by deployed robots, not share of firms that own one. This is conceptually the cleanest fusion: analogous to AEI observed-use coverage layered on task exposure; measures fraction of physical work time being autonomously performed. Inputs: Anthropic/O*NET capability-task weights and BLS employment/hours; a new deployment survey/log dataset by task, with autonomy evidence. High uncertainty initially; response and denominator errors, self-report and reclassification gameability. **Autonomy only verified where independent logs/audits establish no on-task human control/intervention.** Annual pilot survey plus quarterly company/admin samples is feasible. Don’t multiply aggregate 34% physical capability by a broad adoption percentage unless both share the identical task-time denominator and same population; otherwise it is a false precision trap.

### B. Units × productive-use fraction × utilization × relative throughput

`Robot-equivalent physical work hours = N_GP × p_productive × h_year × r`, where `N_GP` is installed eligible units, `p_productive` excludes demos/research/education, `h_year` is productive hours per unit, and `r` is robot output per hour relative to human output on the same tasks. Divide by total physical human-work hours. This gets closer to actual work and permits an order-of-magnitude estimate, but public inputs are missing.

**A deliberately conservative cohort calculation, not a measured deployment estimate:** Epoch’s 2025 humanoid *production* is about 16,000 globally, with the caveat that most purchases are not productive. IDC’s 2025 shipment/use mix (reported in prior project research; original IDC report not publicly accessible here) says >85% were performance, education, data collection or guided tours; Unitree said 9% of Jan–Sep 2025 humanoid revenue was industrial (revenue share, not unit share). Neither gives productive autonomous hours; both are unverified for exact unit/hour interpretation. Use production as an upper-bound proxy for available new units and scenario—not source—assumptions: 1–15% of this cohort productive GP use, 500–2,000 productive hours/year, and 0.1–0.5 human-equivalent output per robot hour (broad range for early deployments, **assumptions, not measured benchmarks**). This yields 8,000 to 2.4 million human-equivalent work-hours/year. Against an approximate world denominator of 2.4 trillion physical work-hours/year (3.5b employed people × 2,000 hours × 34% physical-time share; employed count/annual hours/physical share are rough; 34% is Anthropic’s US estimate and applying it globally is explicitly unverified), that is around **3×10⁻⁹ to 1×10⁻⁶ of physical work time**—roundly 10⁻⁹–10⁻⁶, or 0.0000003%–0.0001%. This is an illustrative cohort bound, not a factual global KPI: it omits prior installed stock and robots of other shapes, while production is not installations or productive units. The wide interval and tiny result mainly expose missing operating data. Using industrial arms/AMRs would raise counts dramatically but violate the GP scope unless task autonomy/generalizability can be screened.

A US-only version is preferable: use US active GP units and US physical-work denominator. Anthropic estimates physical tasks account for 34% of US working hours. A crude denominator is 0.34 × roughly 170m employed × 2,000 annual hours ≈ **116 billion physical hours/year** (employment and annual-hours simplifications; use BLS annual hours/employment series in production implementation). [BLS hours and employment sources](https://www.bls.gov/productivity/technical-notes/industry-hours-and-employment.htm); [Anthropic study](https://www.anthropic.com/research/what-work-can-robots-do). **We do not have a sourced US GP installed-stock or utilization numerator; therefore no defensible US point estimate can be calculated.** Do not convert worldwide production into US deployment or quietly insert a US share. Inputs needed: unit registry/survey, productive-use fraction, uptime and active robot-hours, intervention rate, throughput relative to task-specific human standard. Quarterly company survey/log submissions, annual BLS denominator. Most vulnerable to overcounting idle machines and calling teleoperation autonomy.

### C. Deployed-tier capability share alone

Report share of physical work-time-weighted tasks that have at least one independently documented **productive deployed** GP robot performing the task autonomously in ordinary conditions; optionally tier shares separately. This is an easy interim KPI and is a meaningful lower-bound on capability’s realized frontier—not the fraction of all work automated. Inputs: task ratings × employment/hours; evidence registry. Main bias: one demonstration/site can make a task “deployed” despite trivial hours; broad task labels inflate coverage; audit autonomy and GP transferability. Publish thresholds (e.g. recurring paid production operation, no routine teleoperation) and expose both binary coverage and hours. Annual cadence. **Autonomy verified task-by-task only where evidence supports it.**

### D. Largest verified autonomous deployment records

Maintain leaderboard-style records: biggest independently verified site/task deployment, number of robots, autonomous productive hours, task throughput, intervention/remote-assist rate, utilization, duration and generalization to another site. Report the largest deployment and verified robot-hours, not just a unit count. This is a vivid leading indicator and good case-study stream; it is not an aggregate share and is very gameable via one-off staged tests, selectively reported uptime, or remote assistance. Require third-party audit / operational logs and disclose ODD. Quarterly update; no single global numerator unless sites provide data.

## Recommendation

Do **not** present robot density, shipments, modeled capability, or cost-competitive share as “deployment.” The mature target is estimator A in physical work-time units, underpinned by estimator B’s measured robot-hours and task-specific throughput; use D to validate the numerator. Until coverage is adequate, publish C as the principal provisional KPI, clearly called “share of physical task-hours with verified productive deployment,” plus a small verified-hours panel and confidence range. Maintain Epoch production, IFR density and reported fleet sizes as context/leading indicators, explicitly separate from actual GP autonomous work.

A minimal reporting protocol should count: (1) task/site/industry and generalizability class, (2) paid productive robot-hours excluding charging/idle, (3) task output and human benchmark, (4) all human interventions including remote assistance and exception handling, (5) robot unit and deployment dates, (6) geography and denominator, and (7) independent verification status. Publish supervised, teleoperated and autonomous output separately. This mirrors robotaxis’ paid driverless miles/trips rather than counting vehicles sold.
