# Others’ ideas for measuring general-purpose robot progress

**Research cutoff: 17 October 2026.** Scope is general-purpose autonomous physical work (redeployable humanoids/mobile manipulators), not conventional robot installations as a proxy. “Current” means at the source’s dated publication/capture, not necessarily a continuously updating value. Marketing and forecasts are identified as such. Where I could not inspect a primary source or verify a number, I say so.

## Bottom line

There is no established, recurring, trustworthy public series that measures how much *autonomous, general-purpose* physical labor robots perform. Existing efforts fall into four non-equivalent buckets: (1) industrial/service robot shipments and installed stock (IFR, Stanford AI Index; robust but mostly the wrong robot class); (2) market forecasts and shipment trackers (banks, IDC/analysts, community trackers; count units, often mix shipped, produced, deployed, and operating); (3) task benchmarks and autonomy assessments (Epoch AI, RoboArena, research benchmarks; more capability-relevant but task-specific, episodic, and not a labor-hours series); and (4) surveys/standards (business adoption questions and safety/performance test methods; not a general autonomy outcome measure).

The most promising existing ingredients are Epoch AI’s 2026 task-by-task, evidence-graded review; Metaculus/LEAP forecasting questions with operational definitions; and real-operation logs exposing intervention-free time, task success, throughput, and transfer. “Mean time between interventions” (MTBI) is a compelling proposed operational metric, but I found no standardized cross-vendor, recurring public MTBI series. A single count of robot labor/FTEs would need to exclude teleoperation, supervision, downtime, and narrow purpose-built machines and to be based on independently auditable hours of useful work.

## 1. Official recurring counts: useful context, poor fit

### IFR / World Robotics

- **World Robotics – Industrial Robots** ([IFR landing page](https://ifr.org/wr-industrial-robots/); [IFR definitions and classifications](https://ifr.org/industrial-robots); [World Robotics 2025 executive-summary PDF](https://ifr.org/img/worldrobotics/Executive_Summary_WR_2025_Industrial_Robots.pdf)). Annual, paid report (public executive summaries and releases; detailed country/industry data is paid). IFR reports annual installations/sales and operational stock by country/industry. The 2025 report’s public headline: **542,076 industrial robots installed worldwide in 2024**, second-highest annual total; annual series and installed-stock estimates extend back decades. The definition is an automatically controlled, reprogrammable, multipurpose manipulator programmable in three or more axes, fixed in place or mobile, for industrial automation. This category includes conventional industrial arms and does not isolate redeployable general-purpose autonomy or humanoids. Installation/stock is not evidence of useful autonomous labor.
- **World Robotics – Service Robots** ([IFR page](https://ifr.org/wr-service-robots/); [2025 executive-summary PDF](https://ifr.org/img/worldrobotics/Executive_Summary_WR_2025_Service_Robots.pdf)). Annual survey/report, with public executive summaries and paid detailed data. IFR classifies professional, medical, and consumer service robots by application and follows units sold/shipped, not a general-purpose “robot worker” or autonomy-hours series. The definition is broad: an actuated mechanism programmable in two or more axes, moving within its environment to perform useful tasks for humans/equipment. Application categories include logistics, cleaning, agriculture, inspection, etc.; many are single-purpose. Public 2025 release reports 2024 professional-service robot sales up **9%**, medical up **91%**, consumer-service up **11%**; professional totals and application breakdowns are not a humanoid autonomy measure. I found no verified IFR humanoid time series or a public humanoid-only installed-stock series in the cited 2025 materials. Search results implying IFR sold 7,000 humanoids in 2025 were not corroborated by an IFR primary source; do not treat them as IFR data.
- **Fit:** high-quality recurring context on conventional automation, not the target. Neither IFR series measures task versatility, teleoperation, intervention burden, productivity, or labor displaced. Service robots can include some mobile generalists but aggregate purpose-built systems.

### Stanford AI Index (2025 and 2026)

- **2025 AI Index, Economy chapter** ([chapter PDF](https://hai.stanford.edu/assets/files/hai_ai-index-report-2025_chapter4_final.pdf); [report landing page](https://hai.stanford.edu/ai-index/2025-ai-index-report)). Annual, free, charted historical series sourced from IFR. It reports global **operational stock of industrial robots at 4,282,000 in 2023, versus 3,904,000 in 2022**. The chapter’s related installations/stock graphs inherit IFR scope and history. This is a useful long-run denominator/context series, but no humanoid/general-purpose breakdown, autonomy, intervention or labor-hour accounting.
- **2026 AI Index** ([report page](https://hai.stanford.edu/ai-index/2026-ai-index-report); [Economy chapter PDF](https://hai.stanford.edu/assets/files/ai_index_report_2026_chapter_4_economy.pdf)). Annual, free and historically comparable where definitions remain constant. The Economy chapter again charts industrial robot installations and worldwide operational stock, citing IFR (2025); the accessible search extract labels the stock chart **2012–24**. I could not verify a 2026 AI Index robotics chapter containing humanoid counts or an autonomous physical-work metric. Its robotics coverage remains conventional industrial stock/installation, not embodied-AI capability.

## 2. Capability measurement and robot labor

### Epoch AI

- **“Where autonomy works: evaluating robot capabilities in 2026”** (10 Feb 2026; [full report](https://epoch.ai/publications/where-autonomy-works-evaluating-robot-capabilities-in-2026)). Free, research synthesis rather than a recurring annual numeric index. It specifies **14 concrete tasks** across industrial, household, and navigation domains, assesses public demonstrations/deployments against task definitions, and grades evidence/stage (early research, significant limits in lab, validated in lab, beta/pilot, production deployment). This is unusually direct about autonomy and labor substitution: tasks include package sorting, variable-item picking, laundry, kitchen cleanup, tidying, and navigation. It distinguishes generalist manipulation from fixed-purpose equipment, discusses reliability, speed relative to humans, transfer to new objects/locations, sustained runtime, and human interventions. Examples stated in the report: Figure’s package-sorting demo was a one-hour uncut run at claimed >95% accuracy but roughly **4× slower than an average worker**; warehouse pick-and-place evidence cited as >99% (possibly 99.9%) reliable based on interviews, while human intervention occurs for rare failures; DYNA reported a **24-hour** continuous napkin-folding run, **850+ napkins**, **99.4% success**, **zero human interventions**, at roughly **60% of human speed**; commercial towel-folding claims covered 200,000+ towels across 10 clients in three months. The report carefully separates these claims from broad generality and notes the commercial laundry case is uniform towels, not full household laundry. It concludes navigation is much more mature than manipulation; deployment conditions and transfer are key. Primary research report, but the examples still rely partly on vendor claims and are not a repeated sampling protocol.
- **“How fast could robot production scale up?”** (22 Apr 2026; [report](https://epoch.ai/publications/how-fast-could-robot-production-scale-up)). Free, scenario/reference-class analysis of manufacturing capacity and supply chains, not a measured time series. It estimates 2025 production of roughly **16,000 humanoids** (doubling every six months) and **81,000 quadrupeds** (doubling every ten months), explicitly modeling production not autonomous work. Do not confuse its estimates with audited shipments or active installations.
- **Epoch AI Robotics data insights**: [robot manipulation compute](https://epoch.ai/data-insights/compute-for-robotic-manipulation) (8 Aug 2025) draws on its Robotics dataset and AI Models database. Epoch collects robotics model/data insights, but I found no routinely published leaderboard or annual labor-equivalent count there.
- **Strength/limitation:** best surveyed public evidence for real-world task progress, but a curated snapshot/ordinal rubric rather than a single KPI. Good operational model for an evaluation, not yet a recurring independent dataset.

### METR and “time horizon” for physical tasks

- **METR task-completion time horizons** ([method/series](https://metr.org/time-horizons/); [2025 cross-domain comparison](https://metr.org/blog/2025-07-14-how-does-time-horizon-vary-across-domains/); [paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.14499v3)). METR’s horizon is the human-expert task duration at which an AI agent is predicted to succeed at a specified probability (commonly the **80% time horizon**). It is built from task-level benchmark performance, calibrated human task durations, repeated trials, and fitted success curves. It has a time series for software agents and compared nine benchmarks/domains in 2025, including robotics and self-driving, but the robotics component does **not** establish a comparable, recurring physical-robot horizon series. Key caveat: human task time is not equivalent to robot labor or continuous autonomous uptime; embodied benchmarks differ in hardware, task sets, cost, and evaluation conditions. METR is a transferable *idea*—time-to-complete work at a fixed success probability—not an available KPI for general-purpose physical work today.

### Academic autonomy taxonomies and operational metrics

- **Beer et al., “Toward a Framework for Levels of Robot Autonomy in Human-Robot Interaction”** ([open paper](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5656240/), 2014; older foundational work). Proposes a process and 10-point taxonomy for describing autonomy in context, including robot decision authority and human interaction. It is a classification framework, not a recurring measurement or scalar performance score.
- **Kim & Anthis, “A Taxonomy of Robot Autonomy for Human-Robot Interaction”** ([ACM DOI](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3610977.3634993); 2024). Systematic literature review/taxonomy integrating robot autonomy concepts and HRI. Clarifies that “autonomy” is multidimensional, but is not a public operational benchmark or labor metric.
- **MTBI / intervention rate:** This term is used in HRI/teleoperation and recent deployment commentary. A foundational definition is Fong et al., “A Preliminary Study of Peer-to-Peer Human-Robot Interaction” ([NASA-hosted paper](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20170010160/downloads/20170010160.pdf)): MTBI is average nominal operating time between human interventions, with related “mean time to intervene” and task performance measures. It is a natural measure of how much operator attention is needed. Contemporary robotics discussions explicitly call it a key metric (e.g. [The Robot Report, “Will ‘boring’ robots win in the real world?”](https://www.therobotreport.com/will-boring-robots-win-in-the-real-world-why-robotic-form-should-follow-workflow/), 2026). No generally adopted standard, harmonized definition, independent data collection, or cross-vendor historical MTBI series found. MTBI alone can mislead: intervention severity/latency, safe-stop behavior, robot task value, work rate, uptime, teleoperation, and human supervision must be logged; failure modes and denominator (operating hours vs task attempts) matter.
- **Anthropic “Can we predict the jobs robots will do?”** ([research page](https://www.anthropic.com/research/what-work-can-robots-do), 2026). Proposes a robot-exposure index by mapping occupation work tasks to present-day autonomous robot capability; robots are defined as autonomous physical systems. Measures *potential task exposure*, not actual adoption, autonomous hours, employment displacement, or delivered labor. Free research; a novel bridge to work taxonomy but depends on capability judgments and has no established recurring time series.

### Benchmarks and real-world evaluation

- **RoboArena: Distributed Real-World Evaluation of Generalist Robot Policies** ([paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.18123v1), June 2025; [OpenReview](https://openreview.net/pdf?id=cpmwi3Xwcr)). Free research proposal/framework for distributed real-world evaluation of generalist robot policies across labs/hardware. Focuses on reproducible task performance and policy comparisons in physical robots rather than simulator-only scores. It could produce ongoing capability comparisons if participation and protocols persist; it does not measure deployment-level labor hours or intervention burden by default.
- **RoboChallenge** ([site/leaderboard](https://robochallenge.ai/)), **MolmoSpaces** ([leaderboard](https://molmospaces.allen.ai/leaderboard)), and **RoboDojo** ([2026 paper PDF](https://cdfg.mit.edu/pdf/preprints/2026-chen-robodojo-a-unified-sim-and-real-benchmark-for.pdf)) provide task-level policy benchmarks/leaderboards (some real-world, some sim-and-real). Free/public access to the listed leaderboards/papers; update cadence depends on challenge/model submissions, not an audited recurring economy statistic. Useful for reproducible success rates, not labor impact; simulation and narrow manipulation tasks do not establish autonomy in open-ended jobs.
- **NIST robotics and manipulation** ([Robotics program](https://www.nist.gov/el/robotics); [Robotic Grasping and Manipulation Assembly challenge](https://www.nist.gov/el/intelligent-systems-division-73500/robotic-grasping-and-manipulation-assembly/assembly)). Public test methods/challenges and measurement research for dexterity and task performance. Supports standardized assessment; not a continuously reported general-purpose robot labor metric.

## 3. Market analysts, shipment trackers, and investment theses

These are useful for supply/market context but usually count shipments, production, market revenue, or forecast addressable markets—not robots actually doing autonomous work. Reports are often paid or press summaries paraphrase them; differing definitions make the series non-combinable.

Maps **100 public companies** across the humanoid value chain, primarily a listed-equities thematic basket, not 100 robot makers, units, deployments, capability scores, or labor. Research access may require client/account access; no recurring robot-performance series. The report also presents long-horizon market/economic scenarios. Autonomy: none measured. Its “Humanoid 100” is not a census of operating humanoids.

## 4. Government data, standards, and forecast platforms

### Government adoption / industrial stats

- **US Census Annual Business Survey (ABS) / technology adoption.** ABS provides business adoption data on robotics and other technologies; see Census [ABS program](https://www.census.gov/programs-surveys/abs.html), [technology adoption research page](https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/project/ai-adoption-in-us-census-data/), and Brookings’ explanation of robot-use questions in ABS ([2021 article](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/robot-census-gathering-data-to-improve-policymaking-on-new-technologies/)). Survey is periodic (ABS releases lag by years; technology questions are not a continuous robot telemetry feed). It measures whether firms use robotics, often by industry/size; it does not distinguish humanoid/general-purpose from fixed robots, quantify robot hours or autonomy. Public microdata/table accessibility varies; no verified 2024–26 ABS humanoid series found. Census’s more frequent Business Trends and Outlook Survey (BTOS) tracks AI adoption, not a suitable robotics labor series.
- **China MIIT / national humanoid plan.** MIIT’s 2023 guiding opinion set industrial objectives (humanoid innovation system by 2025 and world-leading development/mass production by 2027); source and translations are available via [USCC report, Oct 2024](https://www.uscc.gov/sites/default/files/2024-10/Humanoid_Robots.pdf) and contemporaneous [Robot Report coverage](https://www.therobotreport.com/china-plans-to-mass-produce-humanoids-by-2025/). These are goals, not measured output or autonomy. A 2026 state announcement describes a humanoid-robot standard system ([SCIO English-language report, 2 Mar 2026](http://english.scio.gov.cn/chinavoices/2026-03/02/content_118354738.html)); it is system-building policy, not an official recurring deployment dataset. Press reports of proposed 100,000 robots/2027 deployment goals or 10,000 robots in use should be treated as targets unless confirmed by official statistical tables with defined scope. I did not find a public MIIT time series of active general-purpose robots or autonomous work hours.
- **EU:** Eurostat/Eurobarometer and enterprise ICT surveys measure business technology use but I found no verified recurring EU dataset isolating general-purpose/humanoid robots, autonomous work, or teleoperator burden. Conventional IFR statistics remain the usable robot count source, with the same scope mismatch.

### Standards and measurement bodies

- **IEEE Humanoid Study Group, “A Pathway Study for Future Humanoid Standards”** ([report PDF](https://www.therobotreport.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/IEEE-Humanoid-Report-of-Future-Standards-Development.pdf), 24 Sep 2025). Cross-sector roadmap, not an adopted performance standard or data series. Identifies likely roles: ASTM test methods, IEEE performance metrics/HRI, ISO safety. Accessible PDF; no historical measure/cadence. It signals standardization interest, not a currently established autonomy metric.
- **ASTM F45 / embodied AI roadmap** ([ASTM white-paper page](https://go.astm.org/white-paper-on-embodied-AI)). ASTM robotics/automation/autonomous-systems committee develops test methods for real-world robotic systems. The 2025 pathway work addresses humanoid performance and safety, but I found no public ASTM F45 recurring “robot labor” statistic or universal MTBI standard. Some standards themselves are paid.
- **ISO:** relevant robot safety standards (including ISO 10218 industrial robots and ISO 13482 personal-care robots) define safety requirements, not a scalar autonomy grade or labor output. Standards are generally paid; no series/cadence. The IEEE pathway study discusses the safety role, not a completed autonomy KPI.
- **NIST:** [robotics program](https://www.nist.gov/el/robotics) develops performance metrics, test methods, and measurement tools for dexterous robot systems; tests/challenges such as [robotic grasping and manipulation assembly](https://www.nist.gov/el/intelligent-systems-division-73500/robotic-grasping-and-manipulation-assembly/assembly) measure bounded task performance. Public resources and challenge updates, not a continuous time series. Autonomy may be a condition of a test, but no population-level labor/autonomy measure.
- **IEEE / NIST ALFUS:** NIST’s [Autonomy Levels for Unmanned Systems framework](https://tsapps.nist.gov/publication/get_pdf.cfm?pub_id=823586) describes autonomy using mission complexity, environmental difficulty, and human independence. It offers a multidimensional classification, not a common robot job score or robot-hours measure. It is not humanoid-specific.

## 5. Explicit claims about the “right” single number

1. **“How long does the robot work before a person steps in?”** MTBI is repeatedly proposed in deployment discourse as more revealing than a polished task demo or raw success rate. It directly probes human attention, but must be paired with productive throughput and intervention taxonomy; otherwise operators can intervene invisibly or definitions can vary.
2. **“How much labor can it actually do?”** Anthropic’s robot-exposure research explicitly asks which job tasks present autonomous robots can perform, mapping capability to occupational tasks. Epoch similarly benchmarks tasks against whether they substitute for a worker, while comparing speed, reliability, transfer, and real deployment. Neither yet measures actual labor hours taken over.
3. **METR’s lesson:** measure a capability horizon against human task duration at a fixed success probability, not vague “intelligence.” Physical work might adopt a defined work portfolio and report the longest task duration completed with ≥80% end-to-end success under autonomy, but differences in hardware and environment make a single score challenging.
4. **IFR/market analysts’ implied number is units shipped/installed.** It is recurring and comparable within its definition, but badly mismatched to the user’s goal: includes fixed-purpose equipment, dormant/pilot units, and human-operated machines; success at shipping is not autonomous work.
5. **Autonomy is context-sensitive.** Autonomy taxonomies (Beer et al.; Kim & Anthis; NIST ALFUS) emphasize task, environment, human role, and decision authority. One scalar may hide whether the robot plans, recovers, transfers to novel jobs, or requires supervision.
6. **Transfer beats demo breadth.** Epoch cautions that demonstrations on fixed setups may be one-off, and highlights limited transfer as the deployment bottleneck. A useful KPI should reward useful work on new objects/tasks/environments, not cumulative number of demo skills.

## 6. Implications for KPI design

No source above supplies the exact KPI sought. A plausible outcome-led measure is **verified autonomous productive hours** (or FTE-equivalent productive hours) by general-purpose physical robots per period, with a strict definition: only useful work in customer/research/operational settings; exclude teleoperation, scheduled human tending, human recovery, and time idle/charging; identify robot class and redeployability; independently audit denominator and interventions. Pair it with **intervention-free productive hours per human support hour** or MTBI and transfer/work breadth. A single number can combine autonomy and substitution only if the work-hour accounting deducts human assistance and excludes fixed-purpose automation; fleet counts cannot.

The data collection would be the hard part: public recurring disclosure from operators/manufacturers, common definitions, independent sampling/audits, and a rolling historical series. Until then, Epoch task evidence plus IFR’s clearly labeled contextual stock and carefully cited shipment trackers offer triangulation, not a defensible single trend line.
